Swarm robotics represents a research paradigm in which multiple robots cooperate to achieve common objectives through decentralized control and local interactions. This approach is inspired by natural systems such as ant colonies, bee swarms, and bird flocks, where complex collective behavior emerges from relatively simple individual agents following basic rules.

Unlike traditional single-robot systems, multi-robot and swarm systems emphasize collaboration, redundancy, and adaptability. Through the conducted literature review, several fundamental characteristics were identified as essential for the success of multi-robot systems:

Coordination: Robots must coordinate their actions to avoid conflicts, share tasks, and achieve collective goals efficiently. Coordination mechanisms may be centralized, decentralized, or hybrid, depending on system scale and application requirements ,.

Communication: Reliable communication is a core requirement, enabling robots to exchange state information, sensor data, and task assignments. Communication limitations directly affect coordination quality and system performance ,.

Scalability: A well-designed swarm system should function effectively with varying numbers of robots. Scalability ensures that adding or removing robots does not significantly degrade system performance ,.

Robustness: Multi-robot systems must tolerate individual robot failures without complete system breakdown. Redundancy and distributed control increase resilience in dynamic or hazardous environments ,.

Swarm robotics is a research paradigm in which multiple robots cooperate to accomplish shared tasks through distributed control and local interactions. The concept is inspired by biological systems such as ant colonies and bee swarms, where complex group behavior emerges from simple rules followed by individual agents rather than centralized intelligence.

Through the literature review conducted in this project, several core principles were identified as essential for the design of effective multi-robot systems. These principles directly influenced the architectural and implementation decisions adopted in our project.

Coordination: Robots must coordinate their actions to avoid conflicts and efficiently achieve shared objectives. In our project, this concept motivated the adoption of a master-centered design, where one robot currently performs mapping and decision-making, with the structure prepared for future multi-robot coordination.

Communication: Reliable communication is critical for information sharing between robots. This requirement influenced our focus on designing a stable communication architecture capable of supporting data exchange between robots and external processing units.

Scalability: Multi-robot systems should maintain functionality as the number of robots increases. This concept guided our use of modular software components and ROS-based design, allowing future extension without major system redesign.

Robustness: A swarm system should continue functioning even if individual robots fail. Although full redundancy is not yet implemented, this principle informed our decision to separate sensing, perception, and control into independent modules to minimize single points of failure.

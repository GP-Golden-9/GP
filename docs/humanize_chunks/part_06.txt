GMapping, which is based on the FastSLAM approach using particle filters, was identified as a strong candidate during the initial literature review. It is widely used within the ROS ecosystem and supports two-dimensional occupancy grid mapping using LiDAR sensors. GMapping benefits from extensive documentation, tutorials, and community support, which makes it reliable and practical for academic and real-world applications. Its proven performance in numerous robotic projects and its suitability for real-time 2D mapping made it a reasonable starting point for the project. However, during the implementation phase, the project transitioned to slam_toolbox, a more modern and actively maintained SLAM framework that provides online and asynchronous mapping modes, better loop closure handling, and finer resolution control. The decision to adopt slam_toolbox was driven by its superior configurability and its ability to produce higher-quality maps at 0.025 m resolution, combined with rf2o laser odometry for scan-based motion estimation on the mapping robot.

Given the scope of the project and the availability of odometry information, GMapping was initially considered due to its flexibility and stronger documentation support, though the final implementation ultimately adopted slam_toolbox for its superior mapping quality and active development.

Additionally, Google’s Cartographer was reviewed to gain insight into more modern SLAM systems. Developed by Google, Cartographer supports both 2D and 3D mapping and offers high accuracy through advanced optimization techniques. However, it has significantly higher computational requirements and increased system complexity. As a result, it was not selected for implementation but was included in the review to understand current trends and advancements in SLAM research,.

A variety of sensor technologies are commonly used to support SLAM in mobile robotic systems. The selection of appropriate sensors has a direct impact on mapping accuracy, localization reliability, and overall system performance.

Two-dimensional LiDAR (2D LiDAR) sensors are widely used in indoor and structured environments. These sensors provide distance measurements in a single horizontal plane, which makes them well suited for detecting walls, obstacles, and free space. Due to their compatibility with occupancy grid mapping techniques, 2D LiDAR sensors are frequently used in ROS-based SLAM systems. In addition, most commercial 2D LiDAR sensors are well supported within the ROS ecosystem, allowing for straightforward integration and real-time data processing. Although range and accuracy vary between different models, 2D LiDAR remains a practical and effective choice for generating reliable maps in indoor search and rescue scenarios.

An Inertial Measurement Unit (IMU) provides information related to orientation, angular velocity, and linear acceleration. IMU data plays an important role in improving localization accuracy, particularly during rotational motion or when wheel slippage occurs. By providing short-term motion estimates, the IMU helps compensate for errors that may arise from wheel encoders alone. When fused with other sensor data, IMU measurements contribute to more stable and accurate pose estimation.

Wheel encoders are another essential sensor for mobile robots, as they measure wheel rotation and enable the estimation of distance traveled and relative motion, commonly referred to as odometry. While wheel encoders provide continuous motion feedback, their measurements are prone to cumulative error over time due to factors such as wheel slip, uneven terrain, and mechanical imperfections. For this reason, encoder data is most effective when combined with additional sensors rather than being used independently.

To overcome the limitations of individual sensors, sensor fusion techniques are commonly employed in SLAM systems. The literature review focused on probabilistic fusion methods, with particular attention given to the Extended Kalman Filter (EKF). EKF-based approaches allow data from multiple sensors—such as wheel encoders and IMUs—to be combined into a single, consistent estimate of the robot’s state.

Within the ROS framework, sensor fusion is commonly implemented using the robot_localization package, which provides EKF-based state estimation for mobile robots. Proper sensor calibration and accurate configuration are critical to achieving reliable fusion results. When correctly implemented, sensor fusion significantly improves localization accuracy and robustness compared to relying on any single sensor, making it well suited for the uncertain and dynamic environments encountered in search and rescue operations. It should be noted that while the EKF-based approach was studied as part of this review, the actual implementation in this project employs a custom complementary encoder and gyroscope odometry fusion for the intervention robot, and rf2o laser-based scan odometry combined with slam_toolbox for the mapping robot, as these approaches better suited the specific hardware configuration and operational requirements of the system.

Object detection has become a critical capability in modern robotic systems, particularly for tasks that require interaction with or identification of objects and humans in the environment. Recent advancements in this field are largely driven by deep learning techniques, especially Convolutional Neural Networks (CNNs), which have demonstrated strong performance in visual perception tasks.

Early object detection methods relied on handcrafted features such as edge detection, color histograms, and texture descriptors. While these approaches were computationally efficient, they were limited in their ability to generalize across varying environments and lighting conditions. A major shift occurred with the introduction of deep learning–based methods, which enabled automatic feature extraction directly from raw image data. Since then, object detection frameworks have evolved to directly predict object classes and bounding boxes in a single pipeline, resulting in significantly improved accuracy and robustness. Modern object detection systems now achieve high performance on standard benchmark datasets and are widely adopted in real-time robotic applications.

In the first literature review, GMapping was selected as a good candidate, because of the FastSLAM method that applies particle filters. It is widely used in the ROS ecosystem and supports two-dimensional occupancy grid mapping with LiDAR sensors. GMapping has good documentation, tutorials and community support, making it reliable and practical for academic and real-world applications. Its proven performance in many robotic projects and its applicability to real-time 2D mapping made it a reasonable starting point for the project. During implementation the project switched to slam_toolbox, a more modern and actively maintained SLAM framework that supports online and asynchronous mapping modes, improved loop closure handling, and finer resolution control. The choice of slam_toolbox was due to its higher configurability as well as the quality of maps generated (resolution of 0.025 m) as compared with the rf2o laser odometry which was used to estimate the scan-based motion of the mapping robot.

The project scope and odometry information availability made GMapping a first choice due to its flexibility and better documentation support. However, slam_toolbox was selected for the final implementation, due to its better mapping quality and active development.

Also reviewed was the Google’s Cartographer to give insight into more modern SLAM systems. The Cartographer from Google supports 2D and 3D mapping with advanced optimisation techniques for high accuracy. However, it requires considerably higher computational resources and increased system complexity. Hence, it was not chosen for implementation but was included in review to understand current trends and advances in SLAM research,

To support SLAM in mobile robotic systems, a variety of sensors are commonly used . The proper selection of sensors directly affects the accuracy of mapping, the robustness of localisation and the overall system performance.

Two-dimensional LiDAR (2D LiDAR) sensors are common in indoor and structured environments. These sensors provide distance measurements in one horizontal plane, which makes them well suited for detection of walls, obstacles and free space. 2D LiDAR sensors are commonly used in SLAM systems based on ROS because they are compatible with occupancy grid mapping techniques. Moreover, the majority of commercial 2D LiDAR sensors are well supported in the ROS ecosystem, which allows easy integration and real-time processing of the data. Although the range and accuracy of different models vary, 2D LiDAR is still a feasible and effective option to generate reliable maps for indoor search and rescue applications.

An Inertial Measurement Unit (IMU) provides information about orientation, angular velocity and linear acceleration. IMU data are important to improve localisation accuracy especially during rotation motion or slipping of wheels. The IMU is used to compensate for errors that can be introduced by wheel encoders alone and provide short term motion estimates. IMU measurements provide more stable and accurate pose estimation when combined with other sensor data.

Wheel encoders are another important class of sensors for mobile robots. They measure the rotation of the wheels, and thus allow the robot to estimate how far it has gone and its relative motion, also known as odometry. Wheel encoders give a continuous feedback of motion, but their measurements can accumulate errors over time as a result of wheel slip, uneven terrain, and mechanical imperfections. That is why encoder data plays well with other sensors than alone.

Sensor fusion techniques are commonly employed in SLAM systems to address the shortcomings of individual sensors. The literature review was conducted specifically on probabilistic fusion methods with special attention to Extended Kalman Filter (EKF). The use of EKF based approaches enables one to combine data from multiple sensors (wheel encoders, IMUs, etc.) into a single, consistent state estimate of the robot.

In the ROS framework, sensor fusion is generally done using the robot_localization package which offers EKF-based state estimation for mobile robots. For reliable fusion results, the sensor must be well calibrated and correctly configured. Sensor fusion, if well implemented, dramatically improves the localisation accuracy and robustness compared to a single sensor, thus being very suitable for the uncertain and dynamic environments faced in search and rescue operations. Although the EKF-based approach was reviewed in this review, the actual implementation in this project was a custom complementary encoder and gyroscope odometry fusion for the intervention robot and rf2o laser-based scan odometry with slam_toolbox for the mapping robot, as these approaches were more suitable for the specific hardware configuration and operational requirements of the system.

Object detection is a core functionality of present day robotic systems, especially for applications that require interaction with or identification of objects and humans in the environment. Recent advances in this area are predominantly driven by deep learning approaches, particularly Convolutional Neural Networks (CNNs), which have demonstrated promising results in visual perception tasks.

Early object detection methods relied on handcrafted features such as edge detection, colour histograms and texture descriptors. These approaches were computationally efficient but lacked the ability to generalise across different environments and lighting conditions. A major transition was the arrival of deep learning based approaches, which allowed automatic extraction of features from raw image data. Object detection frameworks have since evolved to directly predict the object classes and bounding boxes in a single pipeline, leading to significantly improved accuracy and robustness. Modern object detection systems have achieved high performance on standard benchmark datasets and are widely used in real-time robotic applications.

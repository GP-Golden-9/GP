The planning of this graduation project was carried out with a strong focus on practicality, scalability, and alignment with academic objectives. The project aims to develop an autonomous robotic system capable of navigating unknown environments, generating maps, detecting objects, and sensing hazardous gases. Due to the complexity of robotic systems and the limitations of time and resources within an academic setting, careful planning was essential to ensure successful implementation.

Project feasibility was evaluated from multiple perspectives. Technical feasibility was assessed by analyzing the availability of mature robotic frameworks such as ROS 2, proven LiDAR-based mapping techniques, and lightweight deep learning models suitable for real-time object detection. Time feasibility was ensured by dividing the project into manageable phases distributed over an eight-month period. Resource feasibility was considered by selecting hardware components and software tools that are accessible, cost-effective, and well-documented.

The planning process also accounted for future expansion. The system architecture is built around a cooperative multi-robot fleet consisting of three specialized platforms: Alpha (for SLAM and mapping), Beta (for fire detection and intervention), and Gamma (for gas sensing and environmental monitoring). Although the physical evaluation was completed primarily on the Alpha and Beta mobile platforms, the software framework, network protocols, and simulation environments fully support all three robotic profiles in an integrated fashion. This long-term vision influenced early design decisions, particularly in system modularity and communication architecture.

The development of the proposed autonomous robotic system follows an Agile, incremental, and iterative development methodology, which is particularly well-suited for complex cyber-physical systems such as autonomous robots. Robotic systems inherently combine hardware, software, sensing, and control, all of which are subject to continuous refinement as system behavior is observed in real-world conditions. For this reason, a traditional linear development model would impose significant risk, as early design decisions may require revision after integration and testing.

An Agile-oriented approach was selected to allow continuous adaptation and progressive system evolution. Rather than attempting to design and implement the entire system upfront, the project is developed through short, structured iterations that enable frequent validation and refinement. This approach supports rapid feedback cycles, early identification of integration issues, and flexible response to hardware limitations or unexpected system behavior.

The Agile methodology is particularly advantageous for robotics projects, where interactions between mechanical design, sensor accuracy, computational constraints, and environmental conditions cannot be fully predicted at the design stage. By adopting an iterative process, the development team can continuously assess system performance and adjust design decisions accordingly.

The project is divided into multiple development iterations, referred to as sprints, each with clearly defined objectives and deliverables. Each sprint focuses on a specific functional layer of the system, starting from foundational components and progressing toward higher-level autonomous capabilities.

Early sprints were dedicated to mechanical construction, motor integration, and basic robot mobility. Subsequent sprints introduced sensor integration, including LiDAR, camera, and gas sensor setup, followed by the implementation of mapping, localization, and visualization functionalities. Later sprints focused on perception features such as object detection using YOLOv8 (yolov8s.pt primary + fire.pt secondary), dashboard development, and system monitoring.

Each sprint follows a structured lifecycle that includes planning, implementation, testing, and evaluation. At the end of each sprint, implemented features are validated through practical experiments, and identified issues are documented for resolution in subsequent iterations. This structured sprint-based approach ensures continuous progress while maintaining system stability.

The development methodology incorporates principles of systems engineering, ensuring that the robot is treated as an integrated system rather than a collection of independent components. System-level requirements guide the design of individual subsystems, including hardware, software, sensing, and communication.

Throughout development, careful attention is given to subsystem interfaces and data flow. Changes introduced in one component are evaluated for their impact on the overall system, minimizing integration conflicts. This systems engineering perspective ensures coherence between mechanical design, software architecture, and operational objectives.

Additionally, incremental integration is emphasized, where new subsystems are introduced gradually and tested in isolation before being fully integrated into the complete system. This approach reduces integration complexity and improves overall system reliability .

The development team is organized into specialized subteams to enable parallel progress across the different technical domains of the project. This structured organization ensures efficient task distribution while maintaining coherence across the overall system design.

The mechanical and hardware subteam, consisting of two members, is responsible for the physical design and construction of the four-wheel robot platform. Their objectives include chassis design, motor installation, sensor mounting, power management, and ensuring mechanical stability and reliability of the robot.

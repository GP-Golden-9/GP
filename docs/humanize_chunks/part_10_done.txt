The literature review gave us an idea of some of the problems we will face during development as well as providing information for technology and design choices. These include the challenges of electronic circuit design, the iterative nature of mechanical development, the difficulty of integrating multiple heterogeneous sensors, and the dependence of system performance on reliable network infrastructure.

In summary, the literature review provided a solid theoretical foundation for the project by identifying proven technologies, best practices in design and potential challenges. The insights gained were directly used to inform the system requirements and architecture. This provides a clear transition to the system analysis and design phase presented in the following chapter.

The graduation project was designed with a strong focus on practicality, scalability and in line with academic goals. Project is to develop an autonomous robotic system to navigate in an unknown environment, generate maps, detect objects and sense hazardous gases. The complexity of robotic systems, in conjunction with the time and resource constraints of an academic setting, necessitated careful planning for successful implementation.

The project was assessed for its feasibility from various angles. Technical feasibility was assessed in terms of the availability of mature robotic frameworks such as ROS 2, proven LiDAR-based mapping techniques, and lightweight deep learning models for real-time object detection. To assure time feasibility, the project was broken into manageable phases over an eight-month period. The selection of hardware components and software tools was made considering resource feasibility, availability, affordability and good documentation.

Future expansion was also considered in the planning process. The system architecture is based on a cooperative multi-robot fleet, with three specialised platforms: Alpha for SLAM and mapping, Beta for fire detection and intervention, and Gamma for gas sensing and environmental monitoring. The physical evaluation was mainly carried out on the Alpha and Beta mobile platforms, although the software framework, the network protocols and the simulation environments are fully supporting in an integrated way the three robotic profiles. This long-term vision had a great impact on early design decisions, especially on system modularity and communication architecture.

The proposed autonomous robotic system is developed using an Agile, incremental and iterative development methodology which is especially appropriate for complex cyber-physical systems such as autonomous robots. Because the hardware, software, sensing and control are all part of a robotic system, they can be continually improved based on observations of how the system behaves in the real world. So a traditional linear development model would be a real risk as design decisions taken early in the process might have to be re-evaluated after integration and testing.

We used an Agile-oriented approach that allows continuous adaptation and progressive evolution of the system. Instead of trying to design and build the whole system at once, the project is developed in short, well-defined cycles that allow it to be validated and refined regularly. It enables rapid feedback cycles, early discovery of integration issues, and flexible reaction to hardware limitations or unexpected system behaviour.

Agile methodology is especially useful for robotics projects, where the interaction between mechanical design, sensor accuracy, computational constraints and environmental conditions cannot be fully predicted at the design stage. The iterative process allows the development team to continuously evaluate system performance and adjust design decisions as required.

The project is broken down into a number of development iterations called sprints. Each sprint has defined objectives and deliverables. Each sprint focuses on a particular functional layer of the system, beginning with the foundational components and advancing to higher-level autonomous capabilities.

The early sprints were focused on mechanical build, motor integration and basic robot movement. Later sprints included sensor integration, including LiDAR, camera and gas sensor setup, and the implementation of mapping, localisation and visualisation functionalities. The later sprints focused on perception features, such as object detection with YOLOv8 (yolov8s.pt primary + fire.pt secondary), dashboard development and system monitoring.

Each sprint lifecycle comprises well-defined phases such as planning, implementation, testing and evaluation. The implemented features are validated in practical experiments at the end of each sprint, and the identified issues are documented for resolution in subsequent iterations. We use short sprints in a structured way to keep the momentum going and the system stable.

The development methodology is based on systems engineering principles, which guarantees that the robot is not a collection of independent components, but an integrated system. Individual subsystems, including hardware, software, sensing, and communication, are designed based on system requirements.

Throughout development subsystem interfaces and data flow are considered carefully The overall system is checked against changes made to one component in order to minimise integration conflicts. This systems engineering perspective creates consistency between the mechanical design, software architecture, and operational objectives.

Furthermore, emphasis is on incremental integration where new subsystems are added incrementally and tested in isolation before being fully integrated into the overall system. This simplifies integration, and the entire system becomes more reliable.

The development team is divided into specialised subteams, enabling parallel development across different technical domains of the project. This structure enables a proper distribution of tasks, while keeping the coherence of the overall system design.

The mechanical and hardware subteam (2 members) is responsible for the physical design and construction of the four wheel robot platform. Their objectives include chassis design, motor installation, sensor placement, power management, and mechanical stability and reliability of the robot.
